Indian subscription payments are collected by a standing mandate on UPI. When the account is empty on the due date, the payment is declined and the usual answer is to try again tomorrow, and the day after. This agent estimates when the money is likely to arrive, and waits for it.

Why the agent waits

Each day it compares two numbers: the chance the debit clears today, and the best chance available on any remaining day of the cycle. On day 4 those are 12% and 31%, so it holds the attempt. It only has four attempts for the whole cycle, and spending one into an empty account buys nothing.

Where this customer goes wrong

Push the payday uncertainty to ±10 or ±14 days and the agent misses this customer's cycle entirely — it waits past the money and its two attempts both decline. Two other customers in the same population where it does worse are named in scripts/build_page_data.py. One month is an explanation, not evidence; the results below are the evidence.

This customer was picked because the month is easy to read: a flat balance, one salary credit, one debit. Every arrow, wait and decision above was produced by a real agent run at six different levels of payday uncertainty and stored in docs/data/scenarios.json. Nothing is recalculated in the browser.

What it collects, and when the simple version is better

The whole result turns on one thing: how accurately the customer's payday can be guessed in advance. When the guess is good, waiting for the guessed date is hard to beat. When it is not, the agent's estimate of payday — which it learns from the customer's own failed and successful debits — holds up and the guess does not.

What the baseline is

Estimate the customer's payday, wait until that date, then attempt once a day. It is a few lines of code. At ±1 day of error it collects 99.24% and beats the agent by 3.5 points. That row stays on this page because it is the honest comparison, and because it says where this project is not worth building.

How well payday can really be guessed

Nobody knows. No measurement of payday predictability in India was found while building this, which is why it is a slider rather than a constant. The design response was to make the agent learn payday from observed outcomes and report how sure it is, instead of assuming a value.

03

What the agent actually does

Once a day, for every live mandate. A failed debit is treated as information: a declined ₹550 charge says the balance was under ₹550 at that moment, and a successful one says it was at least ₹550. Those two facts are enough to build up a picture of both the balance and the salary date.

The seven steps

  1. Track the belief. One probability distribution per customer, covering the balance and which day of the cycle the salary lands. All of a customer's subscriptions share it.
  2. Compare today with later. If a later day has a better chance, wait.
  3. Diagnose the failure. Name a cause and pick an action: retry, nudge, escalate, stop.
  4. Check the action is legal against five mandate rules.
  5. Execute or refuse. Refused actions never reach the payment rail.
  6. Fold the outcome back in, success or decline.
  7. Log everything, including the days it chose to do nothing.

Two boundaries, enforced in code

A language model never picks the debit time. It explains why a payment failed and chooses between interventions. The data type it returns has no field for a time, and an automated check on the import graph stops that layer from reaching the timing code, the balance estimate or the payment rail at all.

The constraint layer refuses; a separate auditor recounts. One component blocks illegal actions. A second rebuilds what was legal from the audit log alone, using code that is forbidden from importing the first. Both counts are printed side by side — over 8,954 money actions in the headline batch, both are zero.

04

When the payment rail itself is broken

An empty account and a broken payment network produce the same thing: a failed debit. The agent's balance estimate cannot tell them apart, so a rail outage teaches it something false about every customer it touches. Watching the failure rate across all customers at once is how it notices.

What it concluded

Four six-hour outages were injected on days 20, 50, 80 and 110, each knocking out about 40% of attempts. That 40% is a guess — no source reports what fraction of AutoPay debits fail during a UPI incident — so it is swept across three values and never picked. In a separate study with no outage present anywhere, the detector raised a false alarm in 0 of 48 runs; the day-67 alarm above comes from a single run of a horizon that does contain outages, and the two are different measurements.

Noticing is not the same as helping

The obvious response to a detected outage is to stop sending debits into it. Measured across 8 populations, that response loses money at moderate outage severity: it costs a scheduling day whether or not anything is actually wrong, and most of the day's attempts have already gone out by the time the evidence accumulates.

Outage severityPausing is worth2 SE

Pausing therefore does not ship as default behaviour. What does ship is detection plus a change to how declines are folded into the balance estimate, worth +0.256 points at the most extreme severity swept.

And pausing hides the evidence

A paused attempt produces no result, and results are exactly what the detector counts. In the single run shown above, pausing removed enough attempts that the agent caught one outage window instead of two.

05

What an aggregator can see that one merchant cannot

A single merchant charges a customer once a month, so it sees one data point per customer per month. A payment aggregator carrying that customer's other subscriptions sees five. Both learning payday and spotting an outage are volume problems, and volume is the one thing a single merchant cannot buy.

Attempts available to reason about, per 24-hour window

The mandates in this population are spread over 60 merchants. Below 8 attempts, the outage test cannot be evaluated at all.

The vertical mark on each bar is the eight-attempt floor. Below it the agent refuses to evaluate the outage test at all, rather than reading a result off two or three attempts.

The same effect on collection

Letting one customer's subscriptions share a single estimate — rather than each merchant learning that customer separately — is worth +9.53 percentage points (±1.81). It is the strongest argument for running this at the aggregator layer, and the largest single component of the result.

Pooling is also the blind spot

Customers are spread across eight banks. When one bank has an incident, it lifts the pooled failure rate by roughly an eighth of its severity, which is not enough for the test to fire — overwhelming locally, invisible in aggregate.

This rests on an unresolved legal question

Whether a payment aggregator may lawfully use one merchant's transaction outcomes to schedule another merchant's debit for the same customer has not been established. Razorpay's merchant terms, their privacy policy and the relevant RBI directions have not been read. It is recorded in the repository as an open question, not a footnote, because it underpins the strongest claim here.

07

Does this world behave like the real one?

A simulated result cannot be checked by a reader. What can be checked is whether the world producing it reproduces figures published by other people — figures this project did not fit to. There is no public benchmark for payment retry scheduling: no shared dataset, no held-out set, no leaderboard. What exists is aggregate statistics published by companies that sell recovery software. They are second-hand and they aggregate customer bases that are not comparable. They are still the only outside check available, and the world was scored against four of them.

Four published figures, scored not fitted

The world is calibrated against a single anchor. None of the four below was used to set a parameter.

Why two hits matter more than they look

They are properties of different parts of the model. The first is a property of the simulated world — how often an account is short on the due date. The second is a property of a baseline policy running inside that world — what Razorpay's own documented schedule of T, T+1, T+2, T+3 recovers. Two different pieces agreeing with the outside record at the same setting is harder to arrange by accident than one.

The two misses have two different causes

Recovery is too high because no simulated customer is ever simply unable to pay — a clairvoyant scheduler collects 100% here. Recovery is too slow because a mandate's due date and its customer's payday are drawn independently, so the wait between them averages half a cycle. Both are properties of the world, not of the agent. They were once written down as sharing one cause; that was checked and corrected.

Calibrations that score better were found twice, and rejected both times

Adding customers who cannot pay brings the third row inside its band. So does adding temporary holds on the account. Each was swept, and each one broke the first row — the target that currently hits — while doing it. A grid search found a cell satisfying two targets at once; at that cell, both targets it had not been tuned against missed. Two hits obtained by turning two dials are a curve fit with four points, so no recalibration was taken and the reported setting is unchanged. Across fourteen alternative worlds swept so far, none scores better than the one above.

What this cannot establish: the published figures are vendor marketing material, none is ground truth, and a world can reproduce an aggregate statistic while being wrong about the mechanism underneath it. Four numbers is a small suite, and five more targets are specified but not yet built. This is the strongest outside check the project has, and it is not a benchmark.

08

What is simulated, and what is known

No Razorpay transaction, mandate or decline code has been observed by this project. Every percentage and rupee total on this page comes from a simulated world written by the same person who wrote the agent being measured, which is the main threat to all of it.

Simulated

  • Customer balances, salaries and spending
  • Payday timing
  • Every debit outcome
  • Outage windows and their severity
  • The merchant population
  • Every percentage and rupee figure here

From published sources

  • NPCI's five mandate execution rules
  • NPCI's decline code list and what each code means
  • Razorpay's 110 published error reasons
  • Razorpay's documented retry schedule (T, T+1, T+2, T+3, then halt)
  • The shape of Razorpay's Payment Downtime API

Not known

  • How often each decline reason actually occurs
  • How accurately payday can be predicted in India
  • Whether cross-merchant pooling is legally permitted
  • What fraction of debits fail during a UPI incident
  • Whether Razorpay's API accepts the request bodies written here. Requests have now been sent, but they are rejected at authentication before the body is read

The limits worth knowing before quoting anything

  • The headline is conditional on two parameters, not one. At ±1 day of payday error the simple baseline wins by 3.5 points; the +36.66 point figure is measured at ±7 days. It also depends on how hard the world is: the uplift runs +3.51 to +36.43 across the plausible range of spending pressure, and is +6.29 at the setting where this world's due-date failure rate matches the published record.
  • The headline batch runs with the richer decline taxonomy switched off. No frozen accounts, revoked mandates or limit hits. Switching it on costs between 0 and 13.5 points, and every rate in that sweep is a guess.
  • The largest single sensitivity is a guessed constant. How often a debit is refused by a transaction or mandate limit is published nowhere; sweeping it over 0.00 / 0.05 / 0.15 costs 0.00 / −2.87 / −13.46 points. The curve is steeply non-linear, so the middle of the range is not the middle of the damage.
  • The timing score contains a hand-chosen discount. Sweeping it across a plausible range moves the headline over roughly 7 points.
  • Four of the simulation's twenty-five checks are red on a clean checkout, on purpose, each with a written reason rather than a loosened threshold. Two are calibration gates that no parameter can fix; two are controls kept visibly failing so a rewrite stays auditable.
  • Simulation results are not production estimates. The published industry benchmark for retry optimisation is a 6–8% uplift. A simulated +36.66 points is a statement about this world.
  • The study is small. 100 customers, 8 populations, one run seed each.
