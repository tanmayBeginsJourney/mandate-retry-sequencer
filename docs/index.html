Where this wins, and where it loses

The argument is not that the agent is better on average. It is that it does not care how wrong the payday estimate is. The heuristic collapses from 99% to 48% across the same range; the agent stays between 93% and 96%. Below about four days of uncertainty, build the heuristic instead — at ±1 day it beats us by 3.5 points, and that row is on this page for the same reason it is in the repo.

Noticing an outage is worth less than you would like

The agent detects a degraded rail from a burst of technical declines, using an exact binomial tail rather than a normal approximation — at these volumes the expected count is under one, and a z-score turns a single ordinary decline into a one-in-a-thousand event. Detection works. Acting on it does not.

Pausing costs a scheduling day whether or not anything is wrong. It is significantly negative at moderate severity and only turns positive at the most extreme setting swept — which is itself a guess, because no source reports what fraction of AutoPay debits fail during a UPI incident. This is not shipped as an unconditional behaviour.

And pausing hides the evidence it needs

Detector firedattemptstechnicalp

One run, four 6-hour windows at severity 0.40. The published false-alarm rate is 0 of 48 runs measured with no outage present; a firing outside a window in a single run is not that measurement, and both are shown rather than the kinder one.

The thing a single merchant cannot do at all

Mandates are spread across 60 merchants. Pooling every merchant's outcomes for the same customer gives a detector 22.5 attempts per 24-hour window to reason about. One merchant gets 0.38, against a floor of 8 below which the statistic cannot be evaluated. That is not a harder problem for the single merchant; it is an unavailable one.

Detecting a bank-shaped outage

n=200, severity 0.80, four windows, 8 populations. Pooling across banks is what makes the aggregate detector work — and it is also what hides a single-bank incident, because one bank in eight lifts the pooled rate by about an eighth of its severity. Locally overwhelming, statistically invisible.

Two things we learned from Razorpay's own error list

We built a decline taxonomy from NPCI's published code list. Razorpay does not return those codes — it normalises them into 110 reasons of its own. Mapping one onto the other found two states our taxonomy had no name for, and both change what the correct action is.

funds_blocked_by_mandate

The money is in the account and another mandate has already claimed it. Not insufficient funds — the balance is adequate. Not a limit — nothing was breached.

This is cross-merchant contention appearing in the production error vocabulary of a payment aggregator. A merchant who can only see their own debits cannot tell it apart from an empty account, and will retry into a balance that was never theirs to take. It is also the one decline that proves the customer had money at a known moment — the opposite of what a plain failure teaches a balance filter.

deemed_transaction

Every other decline says why the debit failed. This one refuses to say whether it failed. The response was lost; the customer may already have been charged.

Retrying here risks debiting someone twice, which is worse than never collecting — it costs a refund, a complaint and probably the mandate. And it is the cleanest argument on this page for a diagnosis layer: the timing model reads two probabilities and a discount, and there is no arrangement of those three numbers that means “do not act, because the question you are asking is unanswerable.”

Both families are wired into the vocabulary and routed by the diagnosis layer. Neither is simulated and neither carries a number — the frozen world models neither state, and no source gives a frequency for either, so putting a rate on them would be inventing a constant to decorate a good story. What this is not

No real data. Ever. Every number here is simulation. No Razorpay transaction, mandate or decline code has been observed. The world, the policies and the tests were built by one party, which is the failure mode this project has caught itself in twenty-six times and written up each time.

The customer above was chosen because the month is legible — a flat balance, a clean payday, one debit. Others in the same population go worse and two of them are named in the repo. The aggregate table is the evidence; the timeline is the explanation.

Two of the five compliance rules have no working test in the simulation. The attempt cap and the pending-notification rule are enforced by the agent and tested there, but the simulation's own counters for them have never been shown to fire. Peak-hour, notification-lead and re-presentation are genuinely tested.

The batch runs with the decline taxonomy switched off — no frozen accounts, no revoked mandates, no limit hits. Switching them on costs between 0 and 13.5 points, and every rate in that sweep is a guess.
